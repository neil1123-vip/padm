Finding the shape of a route

Maps of familiar streets

5 October 2026 · 4 minute read

A familiar route is not one map. It is a set of shortcuts, landmarks and expectations that overlap while we move. We know where to turn, but we may not know the street's full shape.

Drawing the route from memory makes the gaps visible. A corner can be exact while the distance between corners stretches or shrinks according to how important each place feels.

Draw the landmarks first

Start with the places that make the route recognisable: the low wall, the shop sign, the tree that leans over the pavement. Put them on the page without trying to make the road straight.

  • Use a dot for a place where you usually decide.
  • Use a short line for a stretch you barely notice.
  • Leave a blank space where your memory is uncertain.

The blank space is useful evidence. It shows where attention has been carried by habit rather than by a clear visual record.

Walk the missing section

Take the route again and compare only one gap. Do not try to redraw the entire map while walking. Notice what lies between the landmarks: a change in paving, a doorway, a tree shadow or the sound of traffic behind a wall.

When you return, add the detail to the old drawing in a different colour or with a date. The route now has layers instead of a single claim to accuracy.

A map of a routine records where attention has been spent as much as where the street goes.

Keep the map small

A pocket-sized page is enough. The aim is not to replace a navigation app. It is to make the ordinary route available to memory in a new form.

Next time, draw a different route from the same starting point. The two maps may share a corner but describe different rhythms. That difference is part of what makes a place familiar.
