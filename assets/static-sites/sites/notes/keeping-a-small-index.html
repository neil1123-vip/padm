Keeping a small index

12 October 2026 · 3 minute read

A notebook fills in the order things happen. Finding something later rarely follows that order. You remember the passage about waiting, or the gate at the end of a walk, but not the date on the page.

An index gives those memories another way in. It can fit on a single sheet. Its purpose is to point back to a useful entry, not to summarise everything the notebook contains.

Choose the words you would look for

After a few entries, read them again and underline a handful of concrete words. A subject such as “windows” or “rereading” is easier to recognise than a broad label such as “ideas”. Write the word in the index with a page number or a link.

  • Rereading: a question that changed after a second encounter with a chapter.
  • Light: the patch of shade that made a familiar street look different.
  • Questions: entries with something still worth following up.

One entry can appear under two words. The index does not need a single correct place for each thought. It needs places where you are likely to look.

Index after writing

Leave the original note alone while it is taking shape. Deciding its category too early can pull attention away from what you wanted to say. Add a pointer when you return to the notebook and know which part still matters.

A digital notebook can use a plain list with links. A paper notebook can reserve two pages at the back. Neither needs an elaborate hierarchy. If an index word grows into a long list, add a more specific word beside it rather than reorganising every old entry.

Keep a place for loose ends

Some notes do not belong to a subject yet. Give them a small “return to” list. A pointer and one sentence are enough: “Page 18: find the context of the quoted line.” Resolve an item, keep its answer beside the note, and cross out the pointer.

A useful index remembers where to begin looking. The notebook keeps the rest.

Let the index stay incomplete

Not every grocery list or passing thought needs to be indexed. Start with the entries you have already wanted to find twice. If a forgotten page turns up while browsing, add it then.

This leaves room for two kinds of return: finding a particular note quickly, and opening a page without a plan. Both are useful. A good index makes the first easier without taking away the second.
