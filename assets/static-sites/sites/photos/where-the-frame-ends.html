At the edges

Where the frame ends

7 October 2026

A woodland photograph holds only part of a place. The shapes cut by its boundary help us imagine the rest.

Trace the boundary once

Begin at one corner and follow the four edges without looking at the bright opening. Notice the trunks, branches and patches of foliage that are only partly visible. They tell us that the scene carries on beyond the rectangle.

A partial shape is not necessarily a distraction. It becomes one when its brightness or position pulls attention away from the relationship that makes the photograph work.

Distinguish interruption from clutter

A dark trunk at the edge can hold the frame together. A small bright leaf in the same position can act like a new destination. The difference depends on how the edge relates to the centre, not on whether the object is complete.

Compare the edge with the opening in the canopy. If attention moves back and forth between them, ask whether that movement adds a useful rhythm. Removing every competing detail can make the image calm, but it can also make the woodland feel less expansive.

Try the crop before accepting it

Use two pieces of paper to hide a narrow strip on either side of the image. Keep the central light visible. Then move the strips outward and compare how much the picture seems to extend beyond itself.

Try only one change at a time. A crop that removes an awkward branch may also remove the darker area that gives the light its contrast. Looking at the whole frame again prevents the local correction from becoming the only thing you can see.

A clean boundary and a believable space are not always the same thing.

Leave a reason beside the edit

If you save a tighter version, record what it was intended to do: simplify a small display, emphasise the opening or change the balance of the trunks. Keep the wider source available.

That note makes the crop a decision you can revisit, rather than an unexplained replacement for the original frame.
