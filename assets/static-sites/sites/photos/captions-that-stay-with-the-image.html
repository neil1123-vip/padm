Words beside a picture

Captions that stay with the image

3 October 2026

A caption can give a photograph context without pretending to know more than the picture and its source actually tell us.

Begin with what can be checked

The caption above names a visible relationship: pale snow and darker slopes. It does not assign a season, an exact location or a time of day. Those details could be useful if the source supplied them, but the image alone does not settle them.

Write the visual sentence first. If the context is known, add it in a separate sentence so a reader can tell what was observed and what was documented.

Choose one useful detail

A caption does not need to describe every object. Select the detail that gives the viewer a way into the photograph. For the woodland, that might be the opening in the canopy. For the mountain, it might be the channels traced by snow.

A useful test is to read the sentence, then look back at the image. If you cannot tell where the words point, the caption may be naming a mood rather than a visible feature.

Keep credit and interpretation distinct

The source credit answers a different question from the visual note. Keep it clear even when the caption is short. If the photograph is used in several articles, each page should retain enough information to identify its source.

A caption adds a place to begin looking. It should leave room for the photograph to say more.

Write alternative text for a different purpose

Alternative text should identify the relevant image for somebody who cannot see it. It can name the main forms and their relationship without repeating a long essay or a licence statement.

When a photograph illustrates a specific comparison, include the detail needed for that comparison. The nearby prose can carry the longer interpretation.

Review the caption when the image is cropped or replaced. Words that were accurate beside one version may no longer describe another, even when the file name has stayed the same.
