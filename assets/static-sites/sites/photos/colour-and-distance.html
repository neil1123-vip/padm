Woodland colour

Colour and distance

9 October 2026

Calling this photograph green describes its first impression. Looking at the differences between greens reveals the space inside it.

Start with the lightest patch

Near the opening, leaves are pale and warm. Farther from the light, green becomes darker and less easy to separate from the branches. These changes let the eye distinguish layers even where the shapes overlap.

Choose two small patches that look similar at first. Compare their brightness before their colour. Often the difference in light is what makes one appear nearer, while the colour helps describe the material.

Keep local differences

A uniform increase in saturation would make every green more assertive. It might also make the layers harder to tell apart. The interesting part of the photograph is not how green the whole scene can become, but how one patch differs from its neighbour.

When considering an edit, look at a transition: a lit leaf against shade, a trunk against the canopy or the ground where the sun reaches it. Ask whether the change keeps that relationship readable.

Use a smaller view

Step back from the image until individual leaves become difficult to see. The woodland remains readable as larger areas of light, dark and colour. This is a useful check because it separates the frame's broad organisation from its fine detail.

  • Look for the brightest area first.
  • Follow the darker shapes that divide it.
  • Notice where the colour changes without a hard edge.

Return to a close view afterward. Detail should enrich those larger relationships rather than replace them.

Describe what the colour does

A caption such as “deep green forest” names a mood but leaves little to investigate. “Warm leaves beside the opening, cooler shade behind the trunks” gives a viewer two places to compare.

That comparison is enough. The image does not need an account of the weather or the exact location for its colour relationships to be worth observing.
